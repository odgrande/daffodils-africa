How We Empowered Women-Owned Businesses on International Women's Day 2024

March 2024 · 5 min read

International Women's Day 2024 wasn't just a date on our calendar — it was a call to action. At Daffodils Africa, we believe celebrations mean little without tangible change. So instead of just marking the day, we launched our Social Impact Ajo/Contribution initiative in Lagos, channelling direct, practical support to women-owned businesses in low-income communities.

What Is Social Impact Ajo?

Ajo is a traditional West African savings collective — people pool small amounts regularly and take turns receiving the full pot. We adapted this model for social impact: members of our network collectively contributed funds specifically directed at supporting women entrepreneurs who lacked access to formal credit or business grants.

Who Did We Reach?

Two women-owned micro-businesses in a low-income community in Lagos were selected through a simple nomination and vetting process. Both women were running viable businesses — one a food vendor, one a seamstress — but lacked working capital to grow. The contributions went directly to them, no strings attached beyond a brief business conversation with our team.

What Changed?

One recipient used the funds to stock up on ingredients ahead of a festive period, tripling her usual output. The other invested in materials and a second-hand sewing machine, allowing her to take on larger orders. The impact was immediate and measurable — not in abstract statistics, but in real lives improved before the month was out.

Why This Model Works

Traditional grants are competitive, slow, and often gatekept. Our Ajo model is fast, community-driven, and dignifying. The recipients aren't charity cases — they are entrepreneurs who needed a bridge, and we provided it. We plan to expand this model to more communities in 2025.

5 Things We Learned Running Community Programs in Lagos

2024 · 4 min read

Running community programs in Lagos is one of the most rewarding — and humbling — experiences you can have in social impact work. The city is vast, diverse, and full of surprises. Here are five lessons we carry with us from every project we run.

1. Communities Know Best

We entered our first community programs with plans drawn up in an office. We quickly learned that the people we serve know their own challenges better than any consultant ever could. The most impactful shift we made was listening first, designing second. Co-creation isn't a buzzword — it's the only approach that actually works.

2. Trust Takes Time

Community members have often been promised things that never materialised. Building trust means showing up consistently, keeping small promises, and never overstating what you can deliver. We've learned to under-promise and over-deliver — every time.

3. Logistics Are Half the Battle

Getting supplies, volunteers, and beneficiaries to the same place at the same time in Lagos is an art form. Traffic, weather, last-minute dropouts — we've experienced it all. Detailed logistics planning and always having a Plan B is now non-negotiable for our team.

4. Sustainability Must Be Built In from Day One

The hardest question in community development is: what happens when we leave? We now design every program with an exit strategy from the start — training local leaders, establishing peer networks, and connecting communities with ongoing resources so the impact outlasts our involvement.

5. Celebrate Small Wins

Change is slow. If you only measure success by large outcomes, you'll burn out fast. We've learned to celebrate every milestone: the woman who attended her first skills training, the student who passed an exam, the family whose income improved by 20%. These aren't small wins — they are the work.

CSR

Why Most Corporate CSR Fails — And How to Do It Right

2024 · 6 min read

Corporate Social Responsibility is one of the most misunderstood concepts in the business world. Companies spend billions globally on CSR initiatives — yet most of these programs fail to create lasting change. Here's why, and what the alternative looks like.

The Problem: CSR as PR

Most corporate CSR is designed to look good, not to do good. A one-day tree-planting exercise. A branded food drive before Christmas. These initiatives check the CSR box without addressing root causes, building community capacity, or creating systems that last beyond the photo opportunity.

The Problem: No Expertise, No Partnership

Companies have deep expertise in their industry — not in social impact. Yet most design and run their own CSR programs without partnering with experienced social enterprises or NGOs. The result is well-intentioned but poorly designed interventions that waste resources and, at worst, do harm.

What Effective CSR Looks Like

Effective CSR is strategic, multi-year, and community-centred. It aligns with the company's core business activities — a tech company investing in digital literacy, a food manufacturer addressing food insecurity. It measures outcomes, not just outputs. And it partners with trusted local organisations who already understand the communities being served.

How Daffodils Africa Can Help

We work with corporates and businesses to design CSR programs that actually move the needle. From needs assessment and program design to implementation, monitoring, and impact reporting — we handle the full cycle so your team can focus on what you do best while we do what we do best.

Impact Measurement

How to Measure Social Impact Without a PhD

2024 · 5 min read

Impact measurement has a reputation for being complicated, expensive, and reserved for large organisations with dedicated M&E teams. But measuring what your work actually achieves doesn't have to be that hard. Here's a practical guide for anyone running social programs.

Start with the Right Questions

Before choosing any tool or method, ask: what change are we trying to create? Who is supposed to benefit? How would we know if things are better? These questions sound simple, but answering them honestly is the foundation of all good impact measurement. Most programs fail at measurement because they skip this step.

Outputs vs Outcomes vs Impact

Outputs are what you do (trained 200 women). Outcomes are what changes as a result (80% of them reported increased income 6 months later). Impact is the long-term difference (reduced poverty in the community). Many organisations only measure outputs — which is a start, but not the full story. Aim for at least outcomes.

Simple Tools That Actually Work

Pre/post surveys, beneficiary interviews, community scorecards, and simple tracking spreadsheets can take you a long way. You don't need expensive software to measure meaningful change. What you need is consistency — collect data at the same points, ask the same questions, and compare honestly.

Be Honest About What You Don't Know

Good M&E includes acknowledging gaps. You may not be able to track every beneficiary. Attributing all change to your program is usually inaccurate — other factors are always at play. Honest, qualified reporting builds more credibility than inflated statistics.

Partnerships

The Power of Strategic Partnerships in Social Impact Work

2024 · 4 min read

No organisation changes communities alone. The most transformative social impact work we've seen — and done — has always been the result of intentional, well-structured partnerships. Here's what we've learned about building them right.

Why Partnerships Matter More Than Ever

The challenges facing African communities — poverty, inequality, poor health outcomes, limited education access — are too complex for any single organisation to solve. Effective change requires complementary strengths: local knowledge, funding, technical expertise, community trust, and policy influence. No one organisation has all of these. Partnerships bridge the gaps.

What Makes a Partnership Strategic?

Strategic partnerships are built on shared goals, not just shared logos. Both parties contribute meaningfully. Roles and responsibilities are clear from the start. There is a defined scope and timeline. And there are mechanisms to review, adapt, and hold each other accountable. Anything less is a collaboration in name only.

Our Approach to Partnerships

At Daffodils Africa, we are selective about who we partner with — not because we're exclusive, but because we've learned that partnerships only work when values align. We look for partners who are serious about impact, transparent about their goals, and committed to doing the work, not just being associated with it.

The Result

Our best programs have come from partnerships — with corporates, foundations, community leaders, and government institutions. When we get it right, the effect multiplies: more reach, deeper trust, longer-lasting outcomes.

Women Empowerment

Building Economic Independence for Women in Low-Income Communities

2024 · 6 min read

Financial empowerment isn't just about money — it's about dignity, choice, and the ability to build something lasting. At Daffodils Africa, women's economic independence is at the heart of much of what we do. Here's why it matters and how we approach it.

The Reality We're Working Against

In many low-income Nigerian communities, women carry the heaviest burdens: running households, raising children, often working informal jobs — all while facing barriers to formal employment, credit, and education. The structural disadvantages are real and reinforcing. Changing them requires more than motivation — it requires practical, targeted support.

What Economic Empowerment Actually Means

True economic empowerment isn't just handing someone money. It means building skills so women can generate sustainable income. It means creating access to markets, networks, and opportunities that were previously closed. It means addressing the practical barriers — childcare, transport, time — that prevent women from fully participating in economic life.

Our IWD Ajo Initiative

Our Social Impact Ajo model is one expression of this work. By pooling community contributions and directing them to women entrepreneurs, we create a dignified pathway to capital that doesn't require formal credit history or collateral. The results have been immediate and deeply human.

Skills Training and Business Support

Beyond direct funding, we facilitate skills training programs, business development workshops, and peer networks that help women build sustainable livelihoods. The goal is always self-sufficiency — not dependency on Daffodils Africa, but on skills and systems that outlast our involvement.

Youth

Why Investing in African Youth is Investing in the Future of the Continent

2024 · 5 min read

Africa's greatest resource isn't its land or oil — it's its young people. Over 60% of the continent's population is under 25. How Africa invests in — or fails to invest in — this generation will define the next 50 years. Here's what real investment in youth looks like.

The Scale of the Opportunity

Africa's youth population is the largest and fastest-growing in the world. By 2050, one in four people on Earth will be African. This demographic reality is often called a "youth bulge" — a potential crisis. We prefer to call it what it is: an extraordinary opportunity, if we act now.

The Gaps Are Real

Too many young Africans lack access to quality education, skills training, and meaningful employment. Youth unemployment rates across sub-Saharan Africa remain stubbornly high. Without deliberate investment — in education, mentorship, entrepreneurship, and opportunity — this generation risks being left behind by a global economy that moves faster every year.

What Meaningful Investment Looks Like

It isn't a one-day motivational talk. Real investment in youth means sustained digital skills training, access to internships and mentorship, entrepreneurship support that includes practical business development — not just inspiration. It means creating pathways to employment and self-employment that are realistic and accessible.

Our Digital Literacy Program

Through our digital literacy initiative, we have trained hundreds of young people across Lagos and Abuja in practical digital skills — equipping them to compete in an increasingly technology-driven economy. The results? Participants who secure employment, launch online businesses, and go on to teach others. That is the multiplier effect we're building towards.
